Why Cloud Bills Keep Growing — And the Patterns Behind the Waste

The Flexera 2024 State of the Cloud Report surveyed over 750 cloud decision-makers globally. One of its most consistent findings across five consecutive years of the study: organizations estimate they waste 28% of their cloud spend on average — and they believe the actual number is higher than they can measure (Flexera, 2024).

For a company spending $200,000 per year on cloud infrastructure, that's $56,000 going to resources that aren't delivering value. The figure is consistent enough across organization sizes and industries that it functions as a baseline, not an outlier.

The reason cloud bills grow is almost never one large decision. It's a dozen small ones — provisioning choices that made sense at the time, environments that were supposed to be temporary, and resources that nobody owns anymore. Here's where the waste accumulates.

Abandoned and forgotten resources

Every cloud account accumulates resources from projects that ended. Unattached storage volumes. Old snapshots. Elastic IP addresses not bound to anything. Load balancers pointing at services that were decommissioned. Dev environments from a proof of concept six months ago.

These resources cost money every hour they exist. They don't generate alerts, don't show up in dashboards anyone reviews, and don't have an owner who's motivated to delete them. In Flexera's survey, forgotten and abandoned resources ranked as the top identified source of waste for the fifth consecutive year.

The fix: Enforce resource tagging with owner, project, and expiry date. Any resource without a valid tag or with an owner who has left the organization enters an automated review queue. What doesn't get claimed within 30 days gets stopped, then deleted.

Development environments running 24/7

Dev and staging environments are designed for daytime use. They are almost universally running at 2am on Sunday. Assuming a standard work week (weekday business hours, roughly 9am–7pm), a continuously running dev environment is idle for approximately 65% of the hours it's being billed for.

AWS, Azure, and GCP all support instance scheduling — automated start and stop based on time of day and day of week. This is configuration work, not engineering work, and it typically reduces compute costs for non-production environments by 50–65% without any change to how developers work.

Oversized instances selected for theoretical peak loads

Instance sizing decisions made during initial provisioning tend to be conservative — provisioned for the traffic peak you were planning to hit "by Q3," or the database load that didn't materialize, or the analytics workload that turned out to be lighter than modeled.

All three major cloud providers offer rightsizing tools (AWS Compute Optimizer, Azure Advisor, Google Cloud Recommender) that analyze actual utilization patterns and recommend smaller instance types. The AWS Well-Architected Framework explicitly identifies rightsizing as a foundational cost optimization practice, noting that many workloads run at 10–20% of provisioned capacity on average.

Implementing rightsizing recommendations on clearly underutilized instances — those running below 10% CPU for extended periods — is typically low-risk and produces immediate savings.

Storage tiers that don't match access patterns

Cloud storage isn't a single product — it's a spectrum ranging from millisecond-latency hot storage to archival tiers designed for data accessed once per year. The pricing difference between hot and archival storage is often 5–10x per gigabyte.

Without lifecycle policies, data defaults to hot storage regardless of how often it's actually accessed. Compliance archives, old database backups, and log data from years past are common examples of data that's being stored expensively when it could be stored cheaply.

AWS S3 Intelligent-Tiering, Azure Blob lifecycle management, and GCS Object Lifecycle Management can automate tier transitions based on access frequency. For storage-heavy workloads, this single change frequently reduces storage costs by 30–60%.

Unused Reserved Instances and Savings Plans

Committed pricing — Reserved Instances, Savings Plans, Committed Use Discounts — offers 30–60% savings over on-demand rates for workloads that run continuously. The commitment must be managed actively, however. Organizations that purchased reservations for instance families they no longer use are paying for discounts on resources they've stopped running.

The 2024 Cloud FinOps report by the FinOps Foundation found that coverage rate (percentage of eligible workloads covered by committed pricing) averages around 60% across surveyed organizations — meaning roughly 40% of continuously running workloads are paying on-demand rates when committed pricing is available.

Egress costs from cross-region data movement

Data egress — data leaving the cloud — is charged by the gigabyte in most cloud billing models. The cost is small per unit, which makes it invisible until the aggregate shows up on the bill. Cross-region traffic, data transferred to the public internet, and queries that route through NAT gateways unnecessarily are common sources.

Keeping compute and data in the same region, using VPC endpoints for internal AWS service communication, and reviewing cross-region transfer patterns are standard steps in a cloud cost audit. In organizations that haven't done this review, egress-related waste commonly runs to hundreds or thousands of dollars per month.

The underlying problem: cloud bills require active governance

On-premise infrastructure had a natural governor: physical space, power, and capital budget. You couldn't accidentally provision a server that costs $4,000 per month without someone physically installing hardware. Cloud removes that friction entirely — and with it, the natural checks that kept infrastructure costs bounded.

Replacing those checks requires deliberate process: tagging standards, budget alerts, regular cost review meetings, and designated ownership of the optimization function. The organizations that control cloud costs don't have better engineers — they have better governance.
