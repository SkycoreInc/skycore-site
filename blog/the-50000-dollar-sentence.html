The Hidden Cost of Deferred IT: Why "We'll Deal With It Later" Becomes Your Most Expensive Decision

In 2022, McKinsey published research showing that technical debt — the accumulated cost of deferred IT decisions — consumes between 20 and 40 percent of technology budgets at most organizations, before any new development work begins. Their survey of 1,000+ CIOs found that companies collectively carry over $1.52 trillion in IT debt globally, with the figure growing every year (McKinsey Digital, 2022).

Put differently: for every five dollars a company spends on technology, one to two dollars goes toward maintaining decisions nobody would make today, just to keep them from collapsing.

The phrase that creates this debt is never written down anywhere. It's said in meetings, in Slack threads, in hallway conversations. It sounds like this:

"We'll deal with it later."

Why deferred infrastructure decisions are different from other debt

Financial debt has an interest rate. You know exactly what you're paying to defer. IT infrastructure debt has something worse: compound uncertainty. You don't know the interest rate, and it changes based on what else breaks around it.

Consider what happens when a business defers replacing an aging file server:

  • Month 1: The fix is a straightforward hardware replacement. Estimated cost: $8,000.
  • Month 6: A dependent application has since been updated. The replacement now requires a compatibility project. Cost: $22,000.
  • Month 14: The server fails. Data recovery, emergency procurement, consultant fees, productivity loss, and client impact: $80,000+.

The decision to defer didn't just delay a $8,000 cost — it transformed it into a $80,000 crisis. The "interest" was roughly 900%.

The specific patterns we see most often

Infrastructure debt tends to cluster in predictable places. Based on the businesses we've assessed, these are the most common deferred decisions and how they compound:

End-of-life hardware and software

Running systems past their vendor end-of-life date is one of the most visible forms of infrastructure debt. The Microsoft Windows Server 2012 end-of-support, for example, left thousands of organizations exposed — Shodan scans in late 2023 found over 600,000 Windows Server 2012 instances still publicly accessible months after support ended. Each of those represents a deferred decision that is now actively exploitable, not just theoretically risky.

Undocumented, single-person-dependent systems

The pattern: one engineer built the critical integration, the workflow, or the deployment process — and only they understand it. When they leave (and they always eventually leave), the institutional knowledge leaves with them. What was a manageable dependency becomes a fragile system nobody wants to touch, which creates more deferral, which creates more fragility.

Backup systems that haven't been tested

A backup nobody has tested isn't a backup — it's a theory. The Veeam 2024 Data Protection Trends Report found that 75% of organizations experienced at least one outage in the prior year that required data recovery, yet 58% admitted their recovery capabilities were insufficient. The organizations in that 58% almost always had backups. They just didn't know their backups didn't work until they needed them.

Security patches deferred "until next quarter"

The Ponemon Institute's 2024 State of Vulnerability Management report found that the average time from vulnerability disclosure to exploit in the wild has compressed from 44 days (2021) to under 12 days (2024). Patching quarterly is now functionally the same as patching never, for the vulnerabilities that matter most.

Why smart organizations still defer

It isn't laziness. The structure of most IT organizations makes deferral the rational short-term choice:

  • IT problems don't escalate audibly until they're catastrophic. A slowly degrading server generates no tickets. A failing one generates 200. The quiet problems never feel urgent enough to prioritize.
  • The cost of deferring is invisible; the cost of acting is visible. Nobody puts "avoided crisis" in a budget justification. But they do notice the $22,000 hardware upgrade line item.
  • Ownership is diffuse. If nobody specifically owns the old server, nobody specifically owns the risk of not replacing it.

The structural fix

The solution isn't "be more proactive." That's not a process, it's a wish. What actually changes the outcome:

  1. Make the debt visible. A simple infrastructure register — every system, its age, its support status, its owner, and a rough replacement cost — converts invisible risk into a prioritized list. You can't decide to pay down debt you can't see.
  2. Attach a dollar cost to deferral, not just to action. When a decision is framed as "spend $8K now vs. $0 now," the $0 wins every time. When it's framed as "spend $8K now vs. expected $60K later," the conversation changes.
  3. Test your recovery. Schedule a quarterly restore from backup. Time it. Document the result. It takes two hours and produces a number that is worth more than any other metric in your infrastructure assessment.
  4. Get an outside view annually. The systems your team has lived with for three years are invisible to them in the way water is invisible to a fish. An external infrastructure assessment surfaces what internal familiarity hides.
